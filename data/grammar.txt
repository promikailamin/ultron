*** ENGLISH GRAMMAR AND USAGE CORPUS ***

This corpus explains English grammar, punctuation, spelling, vocabulary, and
writing conventions in clear language with examples. It is used for local
language-model training.

===== Why grammar matters =====

Grammar is the set of rules that explains how words are arranged to make
meaningful sentences. A sentence needs a subject and a predicate. The subject
names the person, place, thing, or idea that the sentence is about. The
predicate says something about the subject and must contain a verb.

Example: The cat sleeps. Here "the cat" is the subject and "sleeps" is the
predicate. The verb "sleeps" tells us what the cat does.

Good grammar helps the reader understand the message quickly. It is not only
about following rules. It is about clarity, rhythm, and respect for the reader.
A sentence can be grammatically correct and still be confusing if it is too
long, too vague, or badly organized.

===== The eight parts of speech =====

English words are often grouped into eight parts of speech: noun, pronoun,
verb, adjective, adverb, preposition, conjunction, and interjection. Many words
can change their part of speech depending on how they are used. The word
"run" can be a verb in "I run every morning" and a noun in "I went for a run."

A noun names a person, place, thing, or idea. Examples: teacher, London,
bicycle, freedom, kindness. Common nouns are general, such as city or book.
Proper nouns name a specific person, place, or organization and are
capitalized, such as Maria, Tokyo, and Minecraft. Concrete nouns name things
you can perceive with your senses, such as apple, music, and wind. Abstract
nouns name ideas or qualities, such as courage, justice, and time.

A pronoun replaces a noun or noun phrase. Examples: I, you, he, she, it, we,
they, me, him, her, us, them, my, your, his, her, its, our, their, mine,
yours, hers, ours, theirs, myself, yourself, himself, herself, itself,
ourselves, themselves. Pronouns must agree with the noun they replace in
number and, in some cases, gender.

A verb expresses an action, an occurrence, or a state of being. Examples: run,
build, think, become, seem, is, are, was, were. Action verbs show physical or
mental action. Linking verbs connect the subject to a word that describes it,
such as "She is happy" or "The soup smells good." Helping verbs, also called
auxiliary verbs, combine with a main verb to form tenses, questions, and
negatives. Examples: do, does, did, have, has, had, be, am, is, are, was,
were, will, would, can, could, may, might, must, shall, should.

An adjective describes or limits a noun or pronoun. Examples: red, tall,
ancient, happy, three, several. Adjectives usually come before the noun, as in
"a red apple," or after a linking verb, as in "the apple is red." Comparative
adjectives compare two things, as in "taller" or "more interesting." Superlative
adjectives compare three or more things, as in "tallest" or "most interesting."

An adverb describes a verb, an adjective, or another adverb. Examples:
quickly, carefully, very, almost, yesterday, here. Adverbs often answer the
questions how, when, where, or to what degree. Many adverbs end in -ly, but not
all do. "Fast" can be an adverb in "He runs fast." "Very" is an adverb in "very
cold."

A preposition shows the relationship between a noun or pronoun and another
word in the sentence. Examples: in, on, at, by, with, under, over, between,
through, during, after, before, about, from, to, of, for. A preposition is
usually followed by an object, which can be a noun, pronoun, or noun phrase.
The group is called a prepositional phrase, as in "under the table" or "after
lunch."

A conjunction connects words, phrases, or clauses. Coordinating conjunctions
join equal parts: for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so. Subordinating conjunctions
join a dependent clause to an independent clause: because, although, if,
when, while, since, unless, until, after, before, though, whereas. Correlative
conjunctions work in pairs: either ... or, neither ... nor, both ... and, not
only ... but also.

An interjection expresses emotion or reaction. Examples: wow, oh, hey, oops,
alas. Interjections are often followed by a comma or an exclamation mark and
are common in conversation and informal writing.

===== Sentence structure =====

A simple sentence contains one independent clause. Example: The dog barked.
A compound sentence contains two or more independent clauses joined by a
coordinating conjunction or a semicolon. Example: The dog barked, and the cat
ran away. A complex sentence contains one independent clause and at least one
dependent clause. Example: When the door opened, the dog barked. A compound-
complex sentence combines both patterns.

Every complete sentence must have a subject and a finite verb. The subject is
usually a noun phrase. The verb must agree with the subject in number and
person. In commands, the subject "you" is understood and often omitted.
Example: Close the door.

Sentences can be classified by purpose. A declarative sentence makes a
statement. An interrogative sentence asks a question. An imperative sentence
gives a command or request. An exclamatory sentence expresses strong emotion.
The punctuation usually matches the purpose.

A phrase is a group of words that works as a unit but does not contain both a
subject and a finite verb. Examples: in the morning, the tall building, to run
quickly. A clause contains a subject and a verb. An independent clause can
stand alone as a sentence. A dependent clause cannot stand alone and usually
begins with a subordinating conjunction or a relative pronoun. Example:
because it was raining.

===== Nouns and pronouns in detail =====

Most nouns form the plural by adding -s, as in book to books. Nouns ending in
-s, -x, -z, -ch, or -sh usually add -es, as in bus to buses, box to boxes, and
watch to watches. Nouns ending in a consonant plus -y change y to i and add
-es, as in city to cities. Many nouns ending in -f or -fe change to -ves, as
in leaf to leaves and knife to knives, but some simply add -s, as in roof to
roofs. Irregular plurals include man to men, woman to women, child to children,
mouse to mice, goose to geese, foot to feet, tooth to teeth, and person to
people.

Possessive nouns show ownership. For most singular nouns, add apostrophe s,
as in the dog's bone. For plural nouns ending in -s, add only an apostrophe,
as in the dogs' bones. For irregular plural nouns, add apostrophe s, as in the
children's toys. Do not use an apostrophe to form a simple plural.

Pronouns change form depending on their role in the sentence. Use subject
pronouns for subjects: I, you, he, she, it, we, they. Use object pronouns
after verbs and prepositions: me, you, him, her, it, us, them. Example: She
gave the book to him. A common mistake is "between you and I"; the correct
form is "between you and me" because "between" is a preposition.

Use "who" for the subject of a clause and "whom" for the object. Example: Who
called you? To whom did you speak? In everyday conversation, "who" is often
used in place of "whom," but formal writing should follow the distinction.

Reflexive pronouns end in -self or -selves and refer back to the subject.
Example: I taught myself to play. Do not use a reflexive pronoun when a plain
pronoun is correct. "Please contact myself" should be "Please contact me."

===== Verbs, tense, and aspect =====

Tense places an action in time. Aspect shows whether the action is complete,
ongoing, or repeated. English has three simple tenses, three progressive
tenses, three perfect tenses, and three perfect progressive tenses.

Simple present: I walk. She walks. Use it for habits, facts, and scheduled
events. Simple past: I walked. Use it for completed actions in the past.
Simple future: I will walk. Use it for actions that will happen later.

Present progressive: I am walking. Use it for actions happening now or around
now. Past progressive: I was walking. Use it for an ongoing action in the past,
often interrupted by another action. Future progressive: I will be walking.

Present perfect: I have walked. Use it for an action that happened at an
unspecified time before now, or for a state that began in the past and
continues. Past perfect: I had walked. Use it for an action completed before
another past action. Future perfect: I will have walked. Use it for an action
that will be completed before a future time.

Present perfect progressive: I have been walking. Past perfect progressive: I
had been walking. Future perfect progressive: I will have been walking.

Regular verbs form the past tense and past participle by adding -ed, as in
walk, walked, walked. Irregular verbs change in other ways, as in go, went,
gone; see, saw, seen; write, wrote, written; take, took, taken; and begin,
began, begun. Learn irregular verbs through examples and repetition.

The subject and verb must agree. A singular subject takes a singular verb, and
a plural subject takes a plural verb. Example: The cat runs. The cats run.
Words between the subject and verb do not change the agreement. Example: The
box of toys is heavy. Indefinite pronouns such as everyone, somebody, each,
and neither are usually singular. Example: Everyone is ready.

Collective nouns such as team, family, and group can take a singular or plural
verb depending on whether the group acts as one unit or as separate members.
In American English, singular agreement is more common. Example: The team is
winning. In British English, plural agreement is often used when the members
act individually.

===== Adjectives and adverbs =====

Adjectives describe nouns. Adverbs describe verbs, adjectives, or other
adverbs. Do not use an adjective where an adverb is needed. Example: She sings
beautifully, not She sings beautiful. Some verbs, called linking verbs, take an
adjective after them because the adjective describes the subject. Example:
The soup tastes good. The word "good" describes the soup, not the act of
tasting.

Comparative forms compare two things. Short adjectives usually add -er, as in
faster. Longer adjectives use "more," as in more comfortable. Superlative forms
compare three or more things. Short adjectives usually add -est, as in fastest.
Longer adjectives use "most," as in most comfortable. Never use both a
comparative ending and "more," as in "more faster." That is a double
comparative and is incorrect.

Some adjectives are absolute and should not normally be compared, such as
unique, perfect, and dead. In informal speech, people sometimes say "more
unique," but careful writers avoid it.

Order of adjectives in English is usually opinion, size, age, shape, color,
origin, material, and purpose. Example: a beautiful small old round blue
French wooden dining table. Native speakers rarely use so many adjectives at
once, but the order explains why "a big red ball" sounds natural and "a red big
ball" sounds strange.

===== Articles and determiners =====

Use "a" before a consonant sound and "an" before a vowel sound. The choice
depends on sound, not spelling. Example: a university, an hour, a European
country, an honest answer. Use "the" when the listener knows which specific
thing is meant, when the noun is unique, or when it has already been mentioned.
Example: I saw a dog. The dog was friendly.

Use no article for general plural nouns and uncountable nouns. Example: Cats
are independent. Water is essential. Use "the" for specific groups or
substances. Example: The cats in my neighborhood are noisy. The water in this
bottle is cold.

Determiners include articles, demonstratives, possessives, quantifiers, and
numbers. Examples: this, that, these, those, my, your, some, any, many, much,
few, little, several, every, each, either, neither, all, both, no. Choose the
determiner that matches whether the noun is countable or uncountable and
whether the meaning is specific or general.

===== Prepositions and common phrases =====

Prepositions can be tricky because usage is often idiomatic. Use "in" for
enclosed spaces, months, years, and long periods. Use "on" for surfaces, days,
and dates. Use "at" for precise times, points, and small locations. Example:
in the box, in July, on Monday, on the table, at noon, at the door.

Common preposition combinations include interested in, good at, afraid of,
proud of, responsible for, depend on, listen to, look at, wait for, arrive at,
arrive in, agree with, agree to, and different from. Some writers use
"different than" in American English, but "different from" is usually safer.

Do not end a sentence with a preposition if doing so creates an awkward
sentence, but ending with a preposition is often natural and correct. Example:
That is the book I was looking for. The rule against ending with a preposition
is not absolute.

===== Conjunctions and sentence combining =====

Use a comma before a coordinating conjunction that joins two independent
clauses. Example: I wanted to go, but it was raining. Do not use a comma if
the conjunction joins only words or phrases. Example: I bought bread and
cheese.

Use a semicolon to join two closely related independent clauses without a
conjunction. Example: The road was icy; we drove slowly. Use a semicolon
before a conjunctive adverb such as however, therefore, or meanwhile when it
joins two independent clauses. Example: The plan was risky; however, it
worked.

Use a colon to introduce a list, an explanation, or a quotation. Example: We
need three things: bread, milk, and eggs. Do not use a colon after a verb or
preposition unless the colon introduces a complete clause or a list.

Avoid comma splices. A comma splice occurs when two independent clauses are
joined only by a comma. Incorrect: I was tired, I went to bed. Correct: I was
tired, so I went to bed. Correct: I was tired; I went to bed. Correct: Because
I was tired, I went to bed.

===== Punctuation =====

A period ends a declarative sentence or an indirect question. A question mark
ends a direct question. An exclamation mark ends an exclamation and should be
used sparingly in formal writing.

A comma separates items in a list, separates introductory elements, sets off
nonessential information, and joins independent clauses with a coordinating
conjunction. The Oxford comma is the comma before the final item in a list of
three or more. Example: red, green, and blue. Many style guides recommend it
because it prevents ambiguity.

An apostrophe shows possession or marks a contraction. Examples: the book's
cover, don't, it's, they're. Do not use apostrophes to form plurals of ordinary
nouns. Use "its" for possession and "it's" for "it is" or "it has." Use "your"
for possession and "you're" for "you are." Use "their" for possession,
"there" for a place, and "they're" for "they are."

Quotation marks enclose direct speech or a quoted passage. In American English,
commas and periods usually go inside the closing quotation mark. In British
English, they often go outside unless they belong to the quotation. Use single
quotation marks for a quotation within a quotation.

A hyphen joins parts of a compound word or phrase, as in well-known or
twenty-one. A dash can set off a parenthetical idea or show a break in thought.
An ellipsis shows omitted words or a trailing thought. Use punctuation to make
meaning clear, not to decorate the page.

===== Capitalization =====

Capitalize the first word of every sentence, proper nouns, the pronoun I,
days of the week, months, holidays, languages, nationalities, geographic names,
and titles when they precede a name. Example: On Monday, Maria and I visited
London. Do not capitalize common nouns, seasons, or directions unless they are
part of a proper name. Example: We drove south in summer.

Capitalize the first word and all important words in a title. Small words such
as a, an, the, and, but, or, and prepositions are usually lowercase unless
they are the first or last word. Example: The Lord of the Rings.

===== Commonly confused words =====

Their means belonging to them. There means a place or a point. They're means
they are. Example: They're putting their books over there.

Your means belonging to you. You're means you are. Example: You're welcome to
bring your friend.

Its means belonging to it. It's means it is or it has. Example: It's raining,
and the dog wants its dinner.

Then refers to time or sequence. Than is used for comparison. Example: We ate
dinner, then we walked farther than before.

Affect is usually a verb meaning to influence. Effect is usually a noun
meaning a result. Example: The weather affects my mood. The effect was
immediate.

Fewer is used with countable nouns. Less is used with uncountable nouns.
Example: fewer apples, less water. In informal speech, "less" is often used
with numbers, as in "less than ten people," but "fewer than ten people" is more
precise.

Lie means to recline or rest. Lay means to put something down. Example: I lie
down to rest. I lay the book on the table. The past tense of lie is lay, and
the past tense of lay is laid. This is confusing even for native speakers.

Who's means who is or who has. Whose shows possession. Example: Who's coming
to dinner? Whose jacket is this?

Could have, should have, and would have are correct. "Could of," "should of,"
and "would of" are incorrect because "of" is not a verb.

Accept means to receive or agree to. Except means excluding. Example: I accept
your apology. Everyone came except Sam.

Advice is a noun. Advise is a verb. Example: She gave me good advice. I advise
you to rest.

Principal means main or a school leader. Principle means a rule or belief.
Example: The principal principle is honesty.

Stationary means not moving. Stationery means writing materials. Example: The
car was stationary. I bought stationery for school.

===== Spelling rules =====

The rule "i before e except after c" is useful but not perfect. Examples:
believe, receive, ceiling, science, weird, seize. Learn common exceptions as
whole words rather than relying only on the rhyme.

When adding a suffix to a word ending in silent e, drop the e if the suffix
begins with a vowel. Example: make to making, hope to hoping. Keep the e if the
suffix begins with a consonant. Example: hope to hopeful. Keep the e in words
such as changeable and noticeable to preserve the soft sound.

When adding a suffix to a one-syllable word ending in a consonant-vowel-
consonant pattern, double the final consonant if the suffix begins with a
vowel. Example: run to running, big to bigger, stop to stopped. Do not double
the consonant if the word has more than one syllable and the stress is not on
the final syllable. Example: visit to visiting.

For words ending in consonant plus y, change y to i before a suffix unless the
suffix begins with i. Example: happy to happiness, carry to carried, but carry
to carrying. For words ending in vowel plus y, keep the y. Example: play to
played.

===== Vocabulary building =====

A strong vocabulary helps you say exactly what you mean. Learn words in
context rather than as isolated lists. Read widely, notice how words combine,
and use a dictionary to check meaning, pronunciation, and usage. Record new
words with an example sentence and review them regularly.

Use precise verbs and nouns instead of vague ones. "Walk" is clear, but
"stroll," "march," "wander," and "hike" give different pictures. "Good" is
general, while "useful," "kind," "reliable," or "delicious" are more specific.

Distinguish between denotation and connotation. Denotation is the dictionary
meaning. Connotation is the feeling or association a word carries. "Cheap" and
"affordable" can describe a low price, but "cheap" may suggest poor quality.
"Childlike" and "childish" both relate to children, but one is positive and the
other is negative.

Avoid unnecessary repetition. Use pronouns, synonyms, and varied sentence
structure to keep writing interesting. Do not replace every word with a
synonym, however, because some synonyms have different shades of meaning.

===== Active and passive voice =====

In active voice, the subject performs the action. Example: The chef cooked the
meal. In passive voice, the subject receives the action. Example: The meal was
cooked by the chef. Active voice is usually clearer and more direct. Passive
voice is useful when the actor is unknown, unimportant, or should be
de-emphasized. Example: The bridge was built in 1900.

To form the passive, use a form of "be" plus the past participle. Example: The
letter was written. The letter has been written. The letter will be written.
Only transitive verbs, which take a direct object, can normally be passive.

===== Questions, negatives, and modals =====

Yes or no questions usually begin with a helping verb. Example: Do you like
tea? Have you eaten? Can she swim? Wh- questions begin with a question word:
who, what, when, where, why, which, whose, how. Example: Where did you go?

To make a negative sentence, use "not" after the first helping verb. If there
is no helping verb, use do, does, or did. Example: She is not ready. She does
not like coffee. She did not call.

Modal verbs include can, could, may, might, must, shall, should, will, and
would. They express ability, permission, possibility, obligation, advice, or
future time. Modal verbs do not add -s in the third person singular. Example:
She can swim, not She cans swim. After a modal verb, use the base form of the
main verb. Example: He must go, not He must goes.

===== Conditionals and reported speech =====

The first conditional describes a real future possibility. Example: If it
rains, we will stay inside. The second conditional describes an unreal or
hypothetical present situation. Example: If I had more time, I would travel.
The third conditional describes an unreal past situation. Example: If I had
studied, I would have passed.

Use "if" for conditions, "unless" for negative conditions, and "when" for
events that are expected. Example: Unless you hurry, you will be late.

In reported speech, tense often shifts back one step. Example: "I am tired,"
she said. She said that she was tired. Time words may also change. Example:
today becomes that day, tomorrow becomes the next day, and here becomes there.
Use pronouns carefully so the meaning stays clear.

===== Writing clear paragraphs =====

A paragraph usually contains one main idea. Start with a topic sentence,
support it with details and examples, and end with a concluding sentence or a
transition. Keep sentences focused. If a sentence contains several unrelated
ideas, split it.

Use transitions to show relationships between ideas: however, therefore, for
example, in addition, meanwhile, as a result, on the other hand, first, next,
finally. Transitions should guide the reader, not decorate the text.

Prefer the active voice, concrete nouns, and strong verbs. Cut unnecessary
words. "Due to the fact that" can become "because." "At this point in time" can
become "now." "In order to" can often become "to." Clear writing is usually
shorter than vague writing.

===== Formal and informal English =====

Informal English is common in conversation, personal messages, and some social
media. It includes contractions, slang, sentence fragments, and casual
punctuation. Formal English is common in academic writing, business documents,
and official communication. It usually avoids slang, uses complete sentences,
and follows standard punctuation.

Choose the register that fits the audience and purpose. A message to a friend
can be relaxed. A job application should be polished. Neither register is
inherently better; each is appropriate in its own setting.

===== Practice examples =====

Incorrect: Me and him went to the store. Correct: He and I went to the store.
The subject form is needed, and the pronoun I comes after the other person.

Incorrect: The report was wrote by the intern. Correct: The report was written
by the intern. The passive voice uses the past participle.

Incorrect: Each of the students have a book. Correct: Each of the students has
a book. "Each" is singular.

Incorrect: She is more taller than her sister. Correct: She is taller than her
sister. Do not combine more with an -er ending.

Incorrect: Their going to the beach with there friends. Correct: They're going
to the beach with their friends.

Incorrect: I have went to that restaurant before. Correct: I have gone to that
restaurant before. Use the past participle with have.

Incorrect: Between you and I, the plan is risky. Correct: Between you and me,
the plan is risky. A preposition takes an object pronoun.

Incorrect: The dog wagged it's tail. Correct: The dog wagged its tail. "Its"
shows possession.

Incorrect: He don't know the answer. Correct: He doesn't know the answer.
Third person singular takes "does not."

Incorrect: We discussed about the problem. Correct: We discussed the problem.
"Discuss" does not need "about."

===== A final checklist =====

Read the sentence aloud. Check that the subject and verb agree. Check that
pronouns refer clearly to the right noun. Check tense consistency. Check
punctuation, especially commas and apostrophes. Check commonly confused words.
Make sure each paragraph has one main idea. Cut words that do not add meaning.

Grammar is a tool for clarity. Rules matter, but meaning matters more. When a
rule and clarity conflict, choose the version that helps the reader understand
you. Revise, read your work again, and ask whether each sentence says exactly
what you intended.
